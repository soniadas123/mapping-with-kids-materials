A GIS Adventure: Exercise 4

Himalayas in 3D

Fly over the highest mountains on Earth using GeoLibre

What you'll use
  • GeoLibre: the same free, web-based GIS tool from the earlier exercises
  • india_boundary.geojson: the outline of India, from Exercise 1
  • No new data! GeoLibre loads the mountain heights by itself.

The Earth Is Not Flat (Even on a Map)

Every map you've made so far has been flat, like a sheet of paper. But the real Earth has mountains, valleys, and plains. Today you'll switch on 3D terrain and see India's ground rise up off the screen.

We'll head to the Himalayas, the highest mountains on Earth, and find out just how high they really are. Then you'll compare them with the place where you live.

GIS word of the day Elevation: how high a place is above the level of the sea, usually measured in metres. The beach is close to 0 m. The top of Mount Everest is 8,849 m.
Fun fact The heights GeoLibre shows were measured from space! A space shuttle and satellites bounced radar signals off the ground to map the height of almost the whole planet. That's remote sensing, just like in the intro.

Here's the plan for today, in seven steps:

  1. Set up a satellite map
  2. Turn on 3D terrain
  3. Tilt and turn the map
  4. Stretch the mountains
  5. Find out how high things are
  6. Measure up and down
  7. Save your best view

1 Set Up a Satellite Map

Mountains look best in a real photo from space.

  1. Open GeoLibre and click Launch GeoLibre Web.
  2. Switch the basemap to Google Satellite, just like in Exercise 1.
  3. Add india_boundary.geojson with a transparent fill and a bright outline, like yellow, so it shows up against the mountains.
  4. Zoom in on the top of India. Look for the white, wrinkled band of snow: those are the Himalayas.
Tip From high above, the Himalayas look flat, like a painting. Keep that in mind; it's about to change!

2 Turn On 3D Terrain

Now let's give the map real height.

  1. Open the Controls menu and turn on Terrain.
  2. Look at the map. Did anything change? Probably not much yet! When you look straight down from above, mountains still look flat.
  3. That's because we need to look from the side. That's the next step.
GIS word of the day Terrain: the shape of the land: its hills, mountains, valleys, and plains. A GIS stores terrain as a grid of elevation numbers, one for each small square of ground. This kind of grid is called a DEM (Digital Elevation Model).
Fun fact A DEM is a type of raster data, the grid-of-squares kind you read about in Exercise 1. Instead of colours, each square holds a height.

3 Tilt and Turn the Map

Time to stop looking down like a bird and start looking across like a pilot.

  1. Tilt: hold Ctrl and drag the map up or down with your mouse (or press Shift + ↑ / ↓). The mountains suddenly stand up!
  2. Turn: hold the right mouse button and drag left or right (or press Shift + ← / →) to spin around the mountains.
  3. Zoom in on a big peak and walk around it by turning slowly.
  4. Lost? Click the compass to point north again.
GIS word of the day Perspective view: looking at the map at an angle, like from an aeroplane window, instead of straight down. Things far away look smaller, just like in real life.
Tip Try finding where the mountains suddenly stop and the flat plains begin. That sharp edge runs right across the north of India.

4 Stretch the Mountains

Even the Himalayas can look a bit small from far away. GIS can make them taller, just for looking.

  1. Double-click the Terrain control to open its setting.
  2. Set the vertical exaggeration to 2. Every mountain is now drawn twice as tall.
  3. Try 3, then 5. How do the mountains look now?
  4. Look at a flat area, like the plains near the Ganga river. Does stretching change it much? Why not?
  5. Set it back to 1 to see the true shape again.
GIS word of the day Vertical exaggeration: stretching heights to make the shape of the land easier to see. It's useful, but remember: it isn't how the land really looks!
Fun fact Stretching flat land does almost nothing. Two times zero is still zero! That's why the plains stay flat while the mountains shoot up.

5 How High Is It?

Let's read the actual heights from the map.

  1. Open the Controls menu and turn on Elevation.
  2. Move your mouse over the map. The height of the ground under your pointer appears in the status bar at the bottom of the screen.

6 Measure Up and Down

In Exercise 2 you measured distances on a flat map. With terrain on, GeoLibre can measure along the ups and downs of the ground too.

  1. Make sure Terrain is still on.
  2. Open Controls → Measure and choose Distance.
  3. Click Start, then click a path across a mountain range: from a valley, over a ridge, and down into the next valley. Double-click to finish.
  4. Compare the normal distance with the Terrain (3D) distance.
  5. Now measure a path of the same length across the flat Ganga plains. Is the difference bigger or smaller?

Fun fact Walking up and down mountains really is longer than the map says. That's one reason a 20 km trek in the Himalayas can take a whole day!

7 Save Your Best View

Find your favourite mountain view and share it.

  1. Pick a peak, valley, or river you like. Tilt and turn until the view looks amazing.
  2. Try Controls → Sun and change the time of day. Watch the shadows stretch across the mountains at sunrise and sunset.
  3. Open Project → Print Layout..., give your map a title, like "Above the Himalayas", and add a north arrow.
  4. Export it as a PNG image.
Tip A scale bar doesn't work well on a tilted 3D view, because things near you look bigger than things far away. That's why we only add a north arrow this time.

You Explored the Roof of the World!

You turned a flat map into a 3D landscape, measured real mountain heights, and saw why walking in the mountains takes so long.

What You Learned

Every place has an elevation above sea level

Looking at the map from the side in perspective view

Reading heights from the map
