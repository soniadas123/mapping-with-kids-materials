Near or Far?

Draw distance zones and ask your map a question, using GeoLibre

What you'll use
  • GeoLibre: the same free, web-based GIS tool from Exercises 1 and 2
  • my_city.csv: a one-row table that you will change to your own city
  • famous_places.csv: the 16 famous places from Exercise 2
  • india_boundary.geojson: the outline of India, from Exercise 1

Maps Can Answer Questions

So far you've used GIS to draw maps. But the real superpower of GIS is asking questions and getting answers from the map.

Today's question is: "Which famous places are near my city?" To answer it, you'll draw zones of a fixed distance around your city, then ask GeoLibre to find every famous place that falls inside them. Everyone in your class can pick a different city, so everyone gets a different answer!

GIS word of the day Spatial analysis: using a map to answer a question about where things are, like "what is close to what?" or "what is inside what?"
Fun fact Real people use these tools every day. Ambulance services check which hospitals are closest, and city planners check which homes are near a new school or park.

Here's the plan for today, in seven steps:

  1. Set up your map
  2. Choose your city
  3. Draw a 400 km zone around it
  4. Make the zone see-through
  5. Ask the map: which places are inside?
  6. Count and compare with bigger zones
  7. Make a layout and export your map

1 Set Up Your Map

Start with everything you already know how to do.

  1. Open GeoLibre and click Launch GeoLibre Web.
  2. Add india_boundary.geojson with Add Data → Vector Layer. Give it a transparent fill and a dark outline.
  3. Add famous_places.csv with Add Data → Delimited Text Layer, using longitude and latitude for the coordinates.
  4. Label the places with their name, so you can tell them apart.
Tip Stuck? Exercise 1 shows how to add and style the India outline, and Exercise 2 shows how to load a CSV and add labels.

2 Choose Your City

Every analysis needs a starting point. Yours will be a city you choose.

  1. Decide on your city. It could be where you live, where your grandparents live, or a city you'd love to visit.
  2. In GeoLibre, find your city on the map, right-click on it, and copy the coordinate.
  3. Open my_city.csv in Notepad. It looks like this:
    name,latitude,longitude
    Bangalore,12.9716,77.5946
  4. Change the second line to your city's name, latitude, and longitude. Save the file.
  5. Load it with Add Data → Delimited Text Layer. Make its dot big and bold so it stands out from the famous places.
Tip Watch the order! The copied coordinate may come as longitude first, then latitude. Latitudes in India are between about 8 and 37. Longitudes are between about 68 and 97. If your dot lands in the sea or another country, try swapping the two numbers.

3 Draw a 400 km Zone

Now let's draw a circle that covers everything within 400 km of your city.

  1. Open Processing → GeoLibre Toolbox → Vector, and choose Buffer.
  2. Set the input layer to my_city.
  3. Set the distance to 400 kilometres. Check the unit carefully!
  4. Click Run. A new layer appears: a big circle around your city.
  5. Rename the new layer to something clear, like zone_400km.
GIS word of the day Buffer: a zone drawn around something at a fixed distance. A buffer around a point is a circle. A buffer around a river would be a long, wiggly band.
Fun fact 400 km is roughly a 7 to 8 hour car journey, or about an hour in a plane. Your zone shows everywhere you could reach "as the crow flies" with that much distance.

4 Make the Zone See-Through

Right now the zone may cover up the famous places underneath it. Let's fix that.

  1. Select the zone_400km layer and open the Style panel.
  2. Pick a fill colour you like, and turn the fill opacity down low, so the zone is see-through like tinted glass.
  3. Give the zone a stronger stroke (outline) in the same colour, so its edge is easy to see.
  4. In the Layers panel, drag the zone below famous_places and my_city, so the dots sit on top.
GIS word of the day Opacity: how solid a colour is. 100% opacity is completely solid; 0% is completely invisible. Somewhere in between lets you see through it.

5 Ask the Map: Which Places Are Inside?

You could count the dots by eye, but GIS can do it for you, perfectly, every time.

  1. Open Processing → GeoLibre Toolbox → Vector, and choose Select by location.
  2. Set the input layer to famous_places. These are the things we want to find.
  3. Set the overlay layer to zone_400km. This is the area we're searching in.
  4. Set the relationship to within.
  5. Click Run. The famous places inside your zone are now selected and highlighted on the map.
GIS word of the day Spatial query: a question you ask a map using location, like "which places are within this zone?" It works just like a search, but for places.
Tip If nothing is selected, that's an answer too! It means no famous places are within 400 km of your city. Bigger zones in the next step will catch some.

7 Make a Layout and Export

Show off your answer with a finished map.

  1. Turn on all three zones, with the biggest at the bottom of the Layers panel and the smallest just under the dots. Give each one a different colour.
  2. Open Project → Print Layout...
  3. Use a title like "Famous Places Near [your city]".
  4. Turn on the legend, north arrow, and scale bar.
  5. Export your map as a PNG or PDF.
Tip Look at the scale bar on your exported map. Does the 400 km ring really look about 400 km from your city? This is a good way to check your work.

Try It Yourself

  • Compare your table with a friend who picked a different city. Whose city has the most famous places nearby?
  • Buffer every famous place by 100 km. Which zones overlap? Which place is the "loneliest", with no other place nearby?
  • Try a tiny zone, like 20 km. Which places are practically next door to each other?
  • Think of a real question you could answer with a buffer, like "which schools are within 2 km of my home?"
