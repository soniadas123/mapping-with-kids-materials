A GIS Adventure: Exercise 2

Famous Places of India

Put landmarks on the map using GeoLibre

What you'll use
  • GeoLibre: the same free, web-based GIS tool from Exercise 1
  • famous_places.csv: a small table of 16 famous places, with their latitude and longitude
  • india_boundary.geojson: the outline of India, from the Exercise 1 data folder

Every Place Has an Address on Earth

Your home has a street address. But every spot on Earth also has a special address made of two numbers, called latitude and longitude. With those two numbers, a GIS can put a dot in exactly the right place on a map.

Today you'll take a simple table of famous places in India, like the Taj Mahal, the Golden Temple, and Kaziranga National Park, and turn it into a map. Then you'll explore the places and measure how far apart they are.

GIS word of the day Latitude and longitude: latitude tells you how far north or south of the equator a place is. Longitude tells you how far east or west it is. Together they are called coordinates.
Fun fact India sits entirely north of the equator, so every place in India has a positive latitude. The southern tip of the mainland, Kanyakumari, is at about 8 degrees north.

Here's the plan for today, in eight steps:

  1. Open GeoLibre and add the India outline
  2. Look inside the table of places
  3. Put the places on the map
  4. Colour the dots by type of place
  5. Write the place names on the map
  6. Explore the places
  7. Measure distances
  8. Make a layout and export your map

1 Open GeoLibre and Add India

First we need a map of India to put our places on. You already know how to do this!

  1. Open GeoLibre in your web browser and click Launch GeoLibre Web.
  2. Click Add Data, choose Vector Layer, and pick india_boundary.geojson from your Exercise 1 data folder.
  3. In the Style panel, set the Fill to transparent and the Stroke to dark grey, width 2, just like in Exercise 1.
  4. Pick a plain, light basemap. Our coloured dots will be easier to see on a calm background.
Tip Forgot a step? Flip back to Exercise 1. Repeating a skill is how real mapmakers get fast.

2 Look Inside the Table

Before we map anything, let's see what our data looks like.

  1. Find famous_places.csv in the Exercise 2 data folder and open it in Notepad or Excel.
  2. Each row is one place. Each column is one fact about it.
  3. Find the latitude and longitude columns. These two numbers are what put each place on the map.
  4. Look at the type column. Every place is a Monument, Palace, Temple, or Nature spot.

Fun fact A CSV file is just plain text with commas between the values. CSV stands for Comma-Separated Values. Open it in Notepad and you'll see the commas!

3 Put the Places on the Map

Now let's turn that table into dots on the map.

  1. Click Add Data. This time, under Files, choose Delimited Text Layer.
  2. Pick famous_places.csv.
  3. Tell GeoLibre which columns hold the coordinates: longitude for X (east-west) and latitude for Y (north-south).
  4. Confirm. Sixteen dots pop up all over India!
GIS word of the day Point data: places shown as single dots. In Exercise 1 you used shapes (polygons) for the country; here each place is so small on a map of India that one dot is enough.

4 Colour the Dots by Type

All the dots look the same right now. Let's give each type of place its own colour.

  1. Click the famous_places layer in the Layers panel. The Style panel opens on the right.
  2. Change Style type to Categorized.
  3. Set Attribute to type.
  4. Choose a Colormap with bright, different colours and click Apply style type.
  5. Make the dots a little bigger so they're easy to spot, and give them a white outline.
GIS word of the day Categorized style: sorting things into groups based on a column, and giving each group its own colour. Here there are four groups, one for each type.

5 Name the Places

Which dot is the Taj Mahal? Let's write the names on the map.

  1. With famous_places selected, find the Labels settings in the Style panel.
  2. Turn labels on and choose name as the label field.
  3. Set the text colour to black and the size to about 12.
  4. Add a white halo about 1.5 wide, so the names are easy to read on top of the map.
  5. Zoom in on Delhi. The Red Fort and Qutub Minar are so close that their names only separate when you zoom in.
GIS word of the day Label: text placed on a map, taken straight from a column in the data. Change the name in the table, and the label changes too.

6 Explore the Places

Our map knows more than just names. Let's find the hidden facts.

  1. Open the Layer actions for famous_places and choose Open attribute table. It's the same table as the CSV, now inside GeoLibre.
  2. Click a row, for example Hawa Mahal. Its dot lights up on the map.
  3. Scroll right to the fun_fact column and read it out loud!
  4. Now right-click on the map near a place and choose What's here? A card pops up with information from Wikipedia.
Fun fact Hawa Mahal means "Palace of Winds". It has 953 small windows, so the royal ladies could watch the street below while staying cool and unseen.

7 How Far Is It?

Maps are great for measuring. Let's find out how far apart some of our places are.

  1. Open Controls → Measure and pick Distance. Set the unit to kilometres.
  2. Click Start. Click once on the Taj Mahal, then double-click on the Gateway of India to finish.
  3. Read the distance. Write it down: ________ km
  4. Click Clear All, then measure from the most northern place (the Golden Temple) to the most southern place (Meenakshi Temple): ________ km
GIS word of the day Straight-line distance: the distance "as the crow flies", in a perfectly straight line. Roads twist and turn, so a car trip is always longer. Check: you should get roughly 1,060 km and 2,430 km.

8 Make a Layout and Export

Time to turn your map into something you can print and show off.

  1. Open Project → Print Layout...
  2. Type "Famous Places of India" as the title.
  3. Turn on the north arrow, the scale bar, and the legend. The legend explains what each dot colour means.
  4. Set the paper to A4, Portrait.
  5. Export your map as a PNG image or a PDF.
GIS word of the day Legend: the key to a map. It tells readers what each colour or symbol means.

Well Done, Explorer!

You turned a plain table into a map, explored it, and measured real distances across India. That's real GIS work!

Try It Yourself

  • Add your own place. Right-click on your school or home in GeoLibre and copy its coordinate. Add a new row to the CSV in Notepad, then load it again. Is your dot in the right place?
  • Which two places on the map are closest together? Measure them to check.
  • Switch the basemap to Google Satellite and zoom right in on the Taj Mahal. Can you see the white dome from space?
  • Pick your favourite place on the map and find three more facts about it.
